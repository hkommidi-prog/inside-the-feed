Inside the Feed

Every scroll is a vote you didn't know you were casting. This site puts you inside Drift, a small fictional social platform, so you can watch its recommendation system decide what gets seen, and, in turn, what gets made.

          Reflection

          What the feed makes

          The question

          This project asks: how do recommendation systems influence which cultural products become visible, popular, and worth creating in the first place?

          I want visitors to notice that a feed is not simply a reflection of what they like. It is also a set of choices about what counts as interest, whose work deserves another chance, and which kinds of posts become safer to make. I made this question playable because these choices can feel abstract until a visitor participates in them: tapping Like, deciding what to post, or moving a slider makes the consequences easier to see.

          The user: personalization as narrowing

          In Part 1, all eight topics begin on equal footing. Each like, watch, or skip changes a topic's weight, and Drift increasingly relies on those weights rather than exploring. The diversity meter describes the spread of possible recommendations; it is not a measure of how broad or narrow a person's actual tastes are. Eli Pariser's The Filter Bubble helped me think about this difference between a person's interests and the version of those interests a platform constructs. A quick skip might mean boredom, but it might also mean bad timing or an unappealing example of a topic. Drift treats it as a signal anyway. Once the system reduces a topic's chances of appearing, the visitor has fewer opportunities to show renewed interest in it. The feed is personalized through the user's actions, but the platform decides how those actions are translated into future choices.

          The creator: a feedback loop

          Part 2 shifts attention from what audiences encounter to what creators feel encouraged to produce. In Dustin Kiskaddon's Blood and Lightning, tattooers' Instagram portfolios are not just records of finished work; the images they choose to show can attract particular clients, whose requests then shape future work and portfolios. Drift turns that feedback loop into a series of posting decisions. An early successful topic brings followers who expect more of that topic. The next time the creator makes something different, the existing audience may respond less strongly, which affects distribution. The comparison with @trendchaser makes the pressure especially visible: following a trend is a plausible growth strategy, even when it pulls a creator away from the work they originally wanted to make. The separate “Feels like your work” score highlights something follower counts cannot measure: creative satisfaction.

          Rankings are never neutral

          The algorithm in Drift has no independent understanding of which post is meaningful, skillful, or valuable. It uses the measures I gave it. An account with many followers is more likely to be selected within its topic because the code weights creators by follower count; that is an advantage built into the ranking rule, not evidence that its posts are better. Safiya Umoja Noble's Algorithms of Oppression challenges the assumption that computational rankings are objective or detached from social power. Her analysis concerns real forms of structural inequality that this fictional model does not reproduce. Tarleton Gillespie's Custodians of the Internet likewise shows how platforms exercise power over visibility through decisions that may seem merely technical. Drift deliberately exposes those decisions instead of presenting a ranking as inevitable.

          The platform: rules as values

          In Part 3, visitors can alter how strongly Drift explores new topics, favors popular accounts, and personalizes a feed. The presets make competing priorities easier to compare, but the results are simulated averages, not predictions about TikTok, Instagram, or any other platform. I would defend a version closer to “Fair discovery” because it gives unfamiliar topics and smaller creators more opportunities to be encountered, even if the model reports less engagement. That is a choice about what a cultural platform should make possible, not proof that variety always makes a better experience for every user. Gillespie's work is useful here because it asks us to treat platform decisions as decisions with public consequences. The sliders are visible in Drift; on many real services, people experience the outcomes without being able to inspect or change the underlying priorities.

          Building as analysis

          Mary Flanagan's Critical Play encouraged me to communicate an argument through the rules of an experience rather than through text alone. Garnet Hertz's Conversations in Critical Making helped me treat the act of constructing those rules as inquiry. For example, I had to assign a numerical effect to liking versus skipping, decide when exploration should fall, and choose the engagement threshold that determines whether a creator's post gets wider distribution. None of those numbers is a universal fact about recommendation systems. Writing them into the model exposed how easily a design can transform an interpretation into an apparently objective result. Adding the “Behind the screen” panel and the Design Notes page was therefore central to the project: the site asks visitors to examine both the consequences of rules and the assumptions behind them.

          Limits of this model

          Drift is intentionally a small, fictional model. Its categories are fixed; its posts are represented by captions and emoji; and simulated people do not have the shifting moods, histories, or complicated social relationships of actual users. The site does not model advertising, moderation, paid promotion, discrimination, or the influence of creators and communities outside a platform. In particular, Noble's account of racialized harm cannot be reduced to Drift's follower-count weighting. The results also include randomness, so a single playthrough should not be treated as a controlled experiment. Simplification is useful because it makes specific feedback loops legible, but it can also mislead if visitors mistake the chosen numbers for measurements of real services. My goal is not to reveal any company's actual algorithm. It is to make the questions behind algorithmic visibility harder to ignore.

          Design notes

          Every rule, disclosed

          Real platforms do not disclose every ranking decision. Drift instead explains the main rules visitors interact with. They are simplified on purpose. The goal is not accuracy to any real platform but making a kind of incentive visible.

          Part 3 · The platform lab

          The lab runs 200 simulated users through 40 posts each, using a modified version of the feed rules from Part 1 with your settings. Exploration starts at 40% and decays toward the floor you choose. Each simulated user loves one random topic (85% chance of liking it) and likes anything else 18% of the time. When "hesitating counts as interest" is on, about a third of their skips are slow skips. Results are averages, so they shift slightly each time.

          Assumptions built into Drift

          • Engagement is the only measure of value. Drift cannot tell a meaningful post from a merely catchy one.
          • Popularity compounds. Accounts that are already large get picked more, which makes them larger.
          • The platform sets the trends. Creators react to trends; they don't choose them.
          • Your past defines your audience. Followers arrive for a topic and expect more of it.
          • Behavior is consent. Drift treats every click and pause as a preference, whether or not you meant it as one.

          What Drift leaves out

          Content moderation, advertising, paid promotion, human curators, real demographic data, and the documented biases that affect whose content is recommended. Each would make the model more realistic and harder to read.

          Ethics and accessibility

          • Interaction choices are processed locally in the browser and are not saved to an account or sent to this site’s server. Google Fonts, when loaded, are requested from an external provider.
          • Creators and posts were written as fictional examples; the model does not use real user profiles.
          • Post images are Unicode emoji and generated color gradients, with no third-party media.
          • The main interactive controls support keyboard input. Meaning is never carried by color alone: bars, results, and badges always include text.
          • Animations are turned off for visitors who set "reduce motion" on their device.

          Making log

          This project began with a simple structure: visitors would first experience the feed as users, then make posting decisions as creators. As the model developed, I added a third part, a platform lab, so visitors could question the rules instead of only living with their effects. Building Drift meant deciding what a “like,” a long watch, a slow skip, a trending format, and a large follower count would mean in numbers. I settled on a feed that gradually reduces exploration, a creator simulation with an engagement threshold, and a lab where those priorities can be changed. None of these values comes from real platform data; each is an illustrative choice. The biggest lesson from turning the idea into code was that every result depends on a design decision made beforehand. Even the dashboard's definition of “diversity” depends on which topics I included and how I chose to count them.

          I tested the site with my mom, dad, and brother. All three were confused at first about the overall goal: they weren't sure what they were supposed to be doing, or what each step was meant to show. Their confusion was useful. Someone who already thinks about recommendation systems fills in the purpose without noticing, but a visitor arriving cold needs each part to explain why it matters, not just what to click. In response, I changed the site's format and rewrote my explanations so that the purpose of each part is clearer before visitors begin. Testing with my family showed me that a critical-play project has to make its stakes clear up front, so that the rules can then do the arguing.
